My Workspace: Fixing a Scattered Workday with AI

My work lived in six tools, and I had quietly become the glue between them. Instead of adding a better to-do app, I traced the cost to its root, the hand-made connections between messages, projects and deadlines, and designed a workspace where AI makes those connections for me.

Target audience
  • Me first: one knowledge worker juggling many projects
  • Then colleagues, through a test-drive copy and a build-your-own guide
My role
I was the problem owner, designer and builder.
I ran the whole cycle: root-cause analysis, risk-based requirements, system design and trade-offs, prompt design, iterative prototyping with an AI partner, and enablement for colleagues.
Tools used
  • Claude (build partner + scheduled tasks)
  • Microsoft 365: Outlook, Teams, OneDrive
  • Electron (HTML/CSS/JavaScript)
?

Problem

My work was spread across six tools, and the only thing connecting them was me:

  • Six disconnected sources. Calendar, email, Teams, a to-do list, notes and a project tracker.
  • Me as the glue. Every morning I matched messages to projects and deadlines by hand.
  • Risks found late. A rescheduled meeting or a new request could quietly put a milestone at risk.

Result: the real work could only start after the morning went into piecing things together.

→
→

Solution

I designed a connected personal workspace that takes over the connecting:

  • One window. Schedule, to-dos, projects, notes and a weekly review together.
  • AI that knows my projects. An hourly task checks new emails and Teams messages against the work I'm tracking.
  • Early warnings, my call. It flags the affected project and suggests a to-do, and I decide what happens.

Result: changes surface before they become problems.

Design Process

1

Find the root cause, not the symptom

The obvious fix was a better to-do app. I didn't trust it, because it treated the symptom (too many tasks) without asking where the time actually went. So I ran a root-cause analysis on my own workday: I listed every place my work lived and traced what happened to a single piece of information from the moment it arrived.

A typical Teams message took four manual steps: read it, work out which project it touched, check whether it moved a deadline, then write a to-do in a different tool. The tasks themselves weren't the bottleneck. The connecting was. And a new to-do app would only have added a seventh place to check.

The reframeThe question changed from "where do I keep my tasks?" to "how do I stop being the person who connects everything by hand?" That one change ruled out a whole category of solutions and pointed every later decision in the same direction.

2

Turn risks into requirements

Giving AI access to my email and calendar is only worth it if it can't cause harm. Before designing a single feature, I listed what could go wrong and turned each risk into a design rule the tool had to follow:

  • AI acting on my behalf → read-only. It can read my calendar, email and Teams, but it can never send, reply or change anything.
  • Data leaving my control → my data, my folder. Everything is stored as plain files in my own OneDrive. No separate server, account or sign-in.
  • Sensitive records reaching AI → blocked at the source. Notes that look like they contain an SSN, ID number, phone number or student email are never sent to AI.
  • AI getting it wrong → humans decide. AI only suggests. Every to-do, alert and project plan waits for me to add, edit or dismiss it.
  • Scope creep → built for one person. Keeping it a personal tool kept it simple and kept other people's data out.

Writing the guardrails first meant they shaped the design instead of being added on at the end.

3

Prototype the smallest useful piece

Rather than specify a whole system I couldn't yet picture, I started with the one screen I'd use every morning: the dashboard. I described it to Claude in plain language and asked for a clickable preview.

From there I used rapid iteration: open the preview, note what was off or missing, share a screenshot, and change one thing at a time. Changing one variable per round made it clear what each change actually did, the same discipline I use when I test a course with learners.

A feature only got built when real use showed a need for it. Quick add came from noticing how often I lost a thought while switching windows, so now I can capture it from any program with one shortcut.

4

Design around the hardest constraint

This was the core of the project. The tool needed live Microsoft 365 data, but my guardrails ruled out the usual route: building a sign-in and an integration that I'd have to secure and maintain myself.

Decision: split reading from displaying

I separated the system into two parts that share only a file. Every weekday hour, a read-only Claude task reads my Outlook calendar, email and Teams and writes a small summary file into my OneDrive folder. The app just reads that file.

This trade-off gave up instant updates for simplicity and safety: the app never holds my credentials or touches my accounts, and either side can change without breaking the other.

Decision: give the AI context, not just content

An AI that summarizes my inbox just gives me a shorter list to read. The insight was to make it do the connecting. I wrote the task's prompt so Claude checks every message against the projects and to-dos already in the tool. When a message touches a project I track, it adds an alert to that project. When action is needed, it suggests a to-do. It also flags schedule conflicts and suspicious emails.

For example, if a colleague writes in Teams that a review has moved to Friday, the dashboard flags that the change squeezes an upcoming feedback milestone and offers a to-do to respond. That's the four manual steps from step 1, done before I open the app.

5

Close the loops inside the tool

Fixing the connections between tools would mean little if the pieces inside the tool still stood alone. I applied the same systems thinking inward, so each part feeds the others:

  • Projects hold the goal, milestones, tasks, notes and documents. When a milestone date moves, its tasks move with it.
  • Plan with AI drafts milestones and tasks from a description or a document, and I review them before anything is created.
  • Notes link to projects, and AI can turn a meeting note into suggested to-dos.
  • Weekly Review shows what got done and how each project moved, closing the loop so I can reflect and adjust.
6

Scale the method, not the tool

When colleagues asked about it, I saw a trap: my tool fits my workflow, and handing it out would just move my solution onto someone else's problem. The part that transfers is the way of thinking. So I designed the rollout as a learning experience:

  • A test drive. Colleagues can try a clean copy with their own data for a few weeks and notice what actually helps them.
  • A build-your-own path. A short guided interview and ready-to-copy prompts for each feature help people build a version around their own workflow.
  • The method, made explicit. I documented the six steps I followed: name the problem, describe it, refine the preview, connect Microsoft 365, package the app, and keep improving.

Why it matters

  • One window instead of six. My day, projects, notes and to-dos live together.
  • Problems surface early. Schedule changes and new requests show up on the project they affect, with a suggested next step.
  • A repeatable method. Colleagues can test-drive the tool or build their own using the prompts and steps I wrote.

The tool is the visible result, but the thinking is what carries over to any problem: question the obvious fix, find the true bottleneck, turn risks into rules before writing features, choose trade-offs on purpose, and build the smallest thing that can prove an idea. It's the same process I use to design learning, applied to my own work.
